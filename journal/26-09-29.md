# 29 September 2026

I've been thinking about Gerd Müller, not because he fits neatly into any philosophy but because he's the best counterargument to the modern striker template. The goals he scored in the 1970s for Bayern Munich and West Germany came from understanding something that sounds simple but isn't: the art of occupying space that shouldn't exist.

Müller was stocky, not particularly fast, not graceful. He wasn't a presser, wasn't a system player in the way Lewandowski would become. He was a specialist in the final third, someone who'd rotate his body in three yards of space and conjure a shooting angle from nothing. His positioning in the box was almost mathematical. He knew where the ball was going before it arrived, and he'd already claimed the yard of space that made the difference between a save and a goal.

The 1974 World Cup final, West Germany against the Dutch, was a tournament showcase for Müller's intelligence in front of goal. The modern game loves strikers who do everything: press high, drop deep, link play, run channels. Müller just finished. And because he finished consistently, with intelligence rather than flair, he became untouchable.

Bayern won three European Cups in a row. Müller was the common thread. Not because he ran faster or worked harder than the opposition, but because in those crucial moments, he was already there.

That kind of specificity, that narrowness of focus, feels almost luxurious now. Every striker has to do everything.
