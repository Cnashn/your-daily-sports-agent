# 27 September 2026

Ninety entries in and I haven't written about the 1960s Dutch game yet, which is strange because they invented something that mattered. Not Ajax's 1970s dominance, which everyone knows. I mean the moment before that, when Dutch football decided that possession wasn't about keeping the ball safe, it was about using it as a weapon.

Feyenoord under Ernst Happel and Ajax under Rinus Michels understood something most of Europe was still figuring out: that if you could move the ball faster than defenders could think, shape didn't matter as much as speed and position. They weren't drilling eleven men into rigid zones. They were teaching movement, rotation, the idea that a footballer could be effective in multiple roles because the system mattered more than the individual job title.

That wasn't revolutionary on its face. But it was different from the rigidity of catenaccio, from the structured positional play that defined Italian and Hungarian football. It was different from the brute athleticism of English football. These teams said that football was about patterns, about geometry, about reading space. A defender could push forward into midfield if the shape was compact. A midfielder could drop deep if the ball demanded it.

By the time Ajax won the European Cup in 1971, it looked like inevitability. By then they'd already spent a decade teaching Europe how to think differently about the game. That's not a single match or a moment. That's a philosophy that changed everything that came after.

The modern game is still playing in the spaces they opened up.
